01Any shape, built from waves

In 1807 the French mathematician Joseph Fourier was studying how heat spreads through metal. Along the way he made a bold claim: any repeating pattern, however jagged, can be built by adding up simple, smooth waves of different sizes and speeds. Some of the best mathematicians of the day, Lagrange among them, did not believe him. Two centuries later it is one of the most used ideas in science.

A drawing is a repeating pattern too: trace the outline, end where you started, and go round again.

02The formula

x(t) = Σ (ak cos kt + bk sin kt)

Read it as a recipe. Each term is a wave that repeats k times per lap; ak and bk say how strong it is. This gives the pen's left–right position x. Its up–down position y gets a recipe of its own.

The trick behind the circles is to write each point as one complex number, x + iy. Then the waves pair up, and each pair becomes a single circle with a fixed radius that spins k times per lap (backwards when k is negative). Put the circles tip to tail and the tip of the last one draws the shape.

On this page, the flag's border, its two stripe lines and the temple outline are joined into one closed path and sampled at 2,048 evenly spaced points. A fast Fourier transform turns those points into 2,048 circles, which are sorted from largest to smallest. Only the N largest are used, and N doubles every lap.

03Why the towers and stairs need so many circles

Big, slow circles make broad, smooth curves. A sharp corner, like the tip of a tower, the edge of a stair or the corner of the flag, turns instantly, and only very small, very fast circles can turn that quickly. With too few of them, corners come out rounded and wobbly.

The wobbles don't spread out evenly. They crowd in right next to the sharp feature. This is the Gibbs phenomenon. Where a signal jumps suddenly, the overshoot never shrinks below about 9% of the jump, however many terms you add; the ripples only squeeze closer to the edge. A corner is gentler than a jump, but its ripples still cluster around it. That is why the error falls quickly at first and then much more slowly, and why the fine details of Angkor Wat appear last.

04The same math is all around you

  • JPEG cuts a photo into 8×8 tiles and describes each one as a sum of cosine waves (a close cousin of Fourier's series). It then drops the fine-detail waves your eye barely notices, which is where most of the space saving comes from.
  • MP3 does the same for sound: it splits audio into frequencies and throws away the ones hidden behind louder sounds.
  • MRI scanners never photograph your body directly. They measure its signal wave by wave, as frequencies, and a Fourier transform turns those measurements into the image.
